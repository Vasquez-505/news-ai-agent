🇪🇺 EU Policy

No significant EU regulations, directives, or decisions were formally adopted or entered into force between November 1 and November 8, 2026.

Close to adoption: The AI Liability Directive. This directive is in the final stages of the legislative process and aims to simplify the process for citizens and businesses to claim damages caused by AI systems. Previously: The burden of proof rested entirely on the claimant to demonstrate the specific causal link between a technical failure in an AI system and the harm suffered (often impossible due to the "black box" nature of AI). Now (upon adoption): A "rebuttable presumption of causality" will be established. If a provider or user failed to comply with the AI Act's transparency or data logging requirements, the court can presume that this failure caused the damage, shifting the burden of proof to the company to prove the AI was not responsible. This will primarily affect AI developers and deployers operating within the EU.

Why it matters: The EU is transitioning from establishing safety and transparency standards via the AI Act to creating the legal and financial frameworks for liability and compensation.

Previously: These entities were not subject to blocking sanctions. Now: All assets within US jurisdiction are frozen, and US persons are prohibited from all transactions with these parties, effective immediately. Previously: Controls focused on a narrow subset of high-end GPUs based on specific Total Processing Performance (TPP) thresholds. Now: The rule includes a broader array of AI-capable accelerators and specific chip-making equipment, effective June 1, 2024. This limits the addressable market for US semiconductor firms specializing in AI hardware. [Commerce.gov]

SEC Cybersecurity Risk Management Rule: The SEC enacted a final rule amending Form 8-K to require public companies to disclose material cybersecurity incidents within four business days of the determination that the incident is material. Previously: No fixed federal deadline existed for reporting material breaches, often resulting in disclosures weeks after an event. Now: A strict four-business-day reporting window is mandated for all public registrants. This increase in transparency is likely to heighten short-term equity volatility following breach announcements. [SEC.gov]

Why it matters: The simultaneous tightening of export controls and the mandate for accelerated cyber-risk disclosures increase regulatory friction and volatility for the US technology and semiconductor sectors.
